Why are people still reading Homer, playing chess and using the wheel, while most apps from ten years ago are gone? One popular answer is the **Lindy effect**: for certain non-perishable things, the longer something has already survived, the longer it can be expected to keep surviving.

## The short definition

The Lindy effect says that for things that don't age biologically — ideas, books, technologies, institutions — **every additional period of survival implies a longer remaining life expectancy**. A book that has been in print for 40 years can be expected to stay in print for roughly another 40; one that has lasted 100 years, for roughly another 100.

That's the opposite of how living things work. A 90-year-old human has *fewer* expected years left than a 20-year-old, because bodies wear out. A book or an idea doesn't have a body to wear out; its survival depends on whether people keep finding it useful.

## Where the name comes from

Lindy's was a delicatessen in New York, famous for cheesecake, where comedians gathered. In a 1964 article in *The New Republic*, Albert Goldman described the "Lindy's Law" discussed there: a comedian's remaining career on television was proportional to how much material they had already used up — so the more they appeared, the sooner they'd burn out.

The idea was then reworked. In 1982 the mathematician **Benoit Mandelbrot**, in *The Fractal Geometry of Nature*, recast it so that a comedian's future career is proportional to their *past* career — longevity predicts more longevity. **Nassim Nicholas Taleb** popularised the modern form in *The Black Swan* (2007) and especially *Antifragile* (2012), applying it to technologies, books and ideas, and coining the shorthand "Lindy" for things that have stood the test of time.

## The maths, briefly

The Lindy effect isn't magic; it falls out of certain probability distributions. If the lifetimes of a class of things follow a **power-law (Pareto) distribution**, then the expected remaining life of something that has already survived for time *t* is proportional to *t*. With a Pareto tail exponent α greater than 1, the expected remaining lifetime is t / (α − 1). If α = 2, something that has lasted 50 years is expected to last another 50.

Compare that with an exponential distribution (like radioactive decay), where the past tells you nothing — the expected remaining life is constant — and with ageing distributions (like human lifespans), where expected remaining life *shrinks* over time.

A related idea is physicist **J. Richard Gott's "Copernican" argument** (published in *Nature* in 1993). If you observe something at a random moment in its life, there's a 50% chance you're in the middle half of its lifespan. That implies, with 50% confidence, that its future duration is between one-third and three times its past duration. Gott famously used this reasoning when visiting the Berlin Wall in 1969 to estimate it would probably stand for under 24 more years; it fell in 1989.

## Everyday examples

- **Books:** Bestseller lists are full of titles that will be forgotten in a few years, while works that have been read for centuries keep getting reprinted.
- **Technology:** Chairs, cups, knives, bicycles and paper have lasted a long time and seem likely to continue. A brand-new gadget category has much less evidence behind it.
- **Software:** Unix-like operating systems, the C language and SQL databases date from the 1970s and are still everywhere — a common argument for choosing "boring", battle-tested tools.
- **Advice and habits:** Taleb applies Lindy to diet and lifestyle, favouring practices with long track records over fashionable new ones.

## Where it breaks down

The Lindy effect is a **heuristic, not a law**, and it's often over-applied.

1. **It only applies to non-perishable things.** Anything with a physical lifespan — people, animals, machines with wearing parts — ages instead.
2. **It's a statistical expectation.** It describes averages across many items, not a guarantee for any single one. Plenty of long-lived things vanish suddenly when the environment changes (think of horse-drawn transport or the telegraph).
3. **Survivorship bias.** We notice the old things that survived, not the many old things that didn't. Age is evidence of robustness, but not proof.
4. **It can't judge genuinely new things.** By definition, new ideas have no track record. Lindy tells you to be sceptical, not that everything new will fail.

## Why it's useful anyway

As a quick filter, Lindy is valuable: when choosing what to read, which tools to rely on, or which claims to trust, giving extra weight to things that have survived a long time is a sensible default. Just remember it's a prior to update — not a substitute for thinking.

## Key takeaways

- The Lindy effect: for non-perishable things, past survival predicts future survival.
- It's named after Lindy's deli in New York and was refined by Mandelbrot and popularised by Taleb.
- Mathematically, it follows from power-law lifetime distributions.
- Use it as a rule of thumb, mindful of survivorship bias and changing environments.
